# RTS Unit & Faction Roster Design — Reference

A breakdown of how classic and modern RTS games structure their unit rosters, aimed at a modern / near-future / semi-sci-fi setting with infantry, vehicles, aircraft and ships.

> **This is genre theory — read it for *why*, not for *what we chose*.** The project's own decisions live in `decisions/` and `docs/factions/`. Where this reference and a project doc disagree, the project doc wins. Two divergences are load-bearing and marked inline below:
> - **ADR `0005-answers-not-holes`** overrides Part 1's non-transitive-matchup framing (we target ~even matchups) and Part 7's hole-heavy worked roster (every faction answers every job). See `docs/factions/CONTEXT.md` and `docs/factions/capability-coverage.md`.
> - The counter matrix Part 4 says to "build as a spreadsheet" now exists as project canon: `docs/factions/counter-matrix.md`.

## Part 1 — Why three factions

> **Superseded on matchup shape (ADR 0005).** We keep three factions for the tuning-cost reason below, but we do **not** aim for the non-transitive "A beats B beats C" triangle this section describes — we target ~even (~50/50) matchups decided in play, not at the select screen. The count argument stands; the triangle does not.

Your instinct about "3 core factions" is correct, and it's not arbitrary. Three is the smallest number that produces **non-transitive matchups** — A beats B, B beats C, C beats A — without any faction being flatly dominant. It's also the largest number most studios can actually hand-tune.

The cost scales badly. Matchups (including mirrors) = *n(n+1)/2*:

| Factions | Matchups to balance |
|---|---|
| 2 | 3 |
| 3 | 6 |
| 4 | 10 |
| 5 | 15 |
| 6 | 21 |

StarCraft has held up for 25+ years partly *because* it only ever had 6 matchups to obsess over. Every one of them got thousands of hours of tuning.

**Precedents:**

| Game | Factions | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| StarCraft / SC2 | 3 (Terran, Zerg, Protoss) | The gold standard for asymmetry |
| C&C: Generals | 3 (USA, China, GLA) | Each faction is a *doctrine*, not a nationality |
| Tempest Rising (2025) | 3 (GDF, Dynasty, Veti) | Two human, one alien — the modern C&C template |
| Red Alert 2 | 2 (+ Yuri in the expansion) | Yuri was added later and is famously hard to balance |
| Age of Empires II | 40+ | Only works because factions are *variations on a shared roster*, not bespoke |

**The lesson from AoE2 vs StarCraft:** you can have many factions *or* deeply bespoke factions. Not both. If you want 3 hand-crafted asymmetric factions, 3 is right. If you ever want 8, they need to share a common unit spine with civ-style modifiers.

### The axis of asymmetry

Factions shouldn't differ by *stats*. They should differ along a stated **axis** you can say in one sentence. Generals is the cleanest example ever shipped:

- **USA** — technology and air power. Expensive, precise, drone-supported, slowest economy.
- **China** — mass and armor. Cheap, slow, bonuses for grouping up, weak air.
- **GLA** — speed and asymmetry. Fragile, fast, stealthy, scavenges wrecks, **no air force at all**.

Note that GLA's defining trait is a *hole in the roster*. That's the single most important faction-design technique in the genre and I'll come back to it in Part 5. *(ADR 0005: we adopt the identity half of this — distinctive, re-profiled answers — but reject removing an actual **answer**. GLA's "no air" is a hole in a capability; our factions differ in how they answer air, never in whether they can. See Part 5 #4 below.)*

Tempest Rising follows the same shape: GDF are high-tech peacekeepers running on an *Intel* resource generated by combat; Dynasty are ruthless attrition-focused industrialists; Veti are aliens who summon buildings rather than construct them and farm their resource sustainably so their fields never deplete.

## Part 2 — The real "core unit" structure

What you're intuiting as "a core unit type for land/air/sea" is actually a **role grid**. Domains (infantry / vehicle / air / naval) are the *rows*. Roles are the *columns*. Almost every RTS roster is this grid, partially filled.

### The universal role list

These recur in essentially every base-building RTS ever made:

| # | Role | Job | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Worker / harvester** | Economy | Defenseless |
| 2 | **Builder / engineer** | Construct, repair, capture | Non-combat |
| 3 | **Scout** | Vision, map control, early harass | Dies to anything real |
| 4 | **Line infantry / trash** | Cheap body, holds ground, garrisons | Loses to armor and AoE |
| 5 | **Anti-armor** | Kills tanks | Fragile, short range, or slow |
| 6 | **Main battle unit** | The generalist backbone (usually the MBT) | Costs real money, beaten by specialists |
| 7 | **Anti-air** | Shoots aircraft | Usually can't shoot ground, or badly |
| 8 | **Artillery / siege** | Outranges static defense | Cannot defend itself, minimum range |
| 9 | **Transport** | Moves army, drops | Unarmed, huge loss when shot down |
| 10 | **Support / utility** | Heal, repair, buff, EMP, jam, shield | No damage of its own |
| 11 | **Detector** | Reveals stealth | Fragile, must be escorted |
| 12 | **Stealth / infiltrator** | Bypasses the front line | Dies instantly to detection |
| 13 | **Hero / commando / specialist** | High-value single unit | Population-capped, expensive |
| 14 | **Superheavy / finisher** | Late-game statement piece | Slow, costly, focus-fired |

You will not fill all 14 slots in all 4 domains. Nobody does. **Roughly 20–30 units per faction is the practical ceiling** before players stop using half of them.

### Real roster sizes for calibration

Tempest Rising's GDF — a deliberately modern, tightly-designed C&C-style roster — breaks down as:

- **6 infantry** (Field Scout, Drone Operator, Engineer, Grenadier, Riot Trooper, Drone Mauler)
- **9 vehicles** (Harvester, Sentinel, Hunter Tank, MCV, Riot Van, Comms Rig, Shieldmaiden, Trebuchet, Scrambler)
- **6 aircraft** (Peacekeeper, Skycrane, Queller, Condor, Drone Control Unit, plus drones)
- **5 specialists** (Sniper, Comms Officer, Riot Medic, Drone Assassin, Hijacker)
- **0 naval**

That's ~26 buildable units across **3 tech tiers**, with no navy at all. The third faction (Veti) shipped with a comparable count: 6 infantry, 7 vehicles, 5 air, 5 specialists.

StarCraft II sits around 16–18 units per race. Generals is around 20. **If you're targeting 3 factions × 4 domains, you're looking at 60–90 units total, and that is a genuinely large project.** Consider whether you need all four domains (see Part 6).

## Part 3 — Domain-by-domain slot templates

### Infantry

Infantry are cheap, produced fast, and exist to hold ground and provide utility. In modern-setting RTS they usually also **garrison buildings**, which is what keeps them relevant past the ten-minute mark.

| Slot | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Basic rifleman | Cheap body, garrisons, mass-produced | GI, Conscript, Marine, Field Scout, Red Guard |
| AT trooper | Rocket/missile infantry, kills vehicles | Drone Mauler, Tank Hunter, Missile Defender |
| AA trooper | Dedicated air denial | Flak Trooper, Rocketeer (also AA), Stinger crews |
| Engineer | Repair, capture buildings, defuse | Engineer (universal — nearly every C&C has one) |
| Heavy / elite | Tanky frontliner, often deployable | Guardian GI, Tesla Trooper, Riot Trooper |
| AoE specialist | Grenades/flamer, clears garrisons | Grenadier, Flame Trooper, Crazy Ivan |
| Sniper | Kills infantry at extreme range, detector | Sniper, Pathfinder, Navy SEAL |
| Commando / hero | One-off high-impact unit | Tanya, Colonel Burton, Boris |
| Infiltrator | Stealth, sabotage, mind control | Spy, Chrono Legionnaire, Hijacker |

**Design note — the AT/AA split.** A common efficiency move is folding anti-tank and anti-air into one unit. Tempest Rising's GDF Drone Operator is explicitly *"anti-vehicle and anti-air"*. This saves a slot but makes that unit mandatory in every composition, which flattens decision-making. Splitting them (RA2's Flak Trooper vs Tesla Trooper) forces real composition choices. Pick deliberately.

### Vehicles

The vehicle bay is where most RTS actually get decided. This is your deepest tree.

| Slot | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Harvester | Economy | War Miner, Chrono Miner, Supply Truck |
| MCV / mobile base | Expansion, base relocation | MCV (C&C staple) |
| Scout vehicle | Fast, cheap, vision, raids | Sentinel, Humvee, Attack Bike |
| **Main battle tank** | The backbone | Grizzly, Rhino, Hunter Tank, Crusader |
| Light AT / tank destroyer | Glass cannon, kills MBTs | Rocket Buggy, Scrambler |
| APC / transport | Moves infantry, often a weapon platform | IFV, Flak Track, Riot Van |
| Mobile AA | Protects the ball from air | Flak Track, Avenger, Gatling Tank |
| Artillery | Long range, sieges bases, deploys to fire | V3 Launcher, Trebuchet, Nuke Cannon |
| Support vehicle | Repair, jam, mine-lay, buff | Comms Rig, Battle Drone, Shieldmaiden |
| Superheavy | The big one | Apocalypse Tank, Overlord Tank |

**The MBT is the single most important unit in your game.** It's the unit players build by default, the unit that defines whether your game feels heavy or nimble, and the unit every other unit is priced against. RA2 does the comparison perfectly: the Allied **Grizzly** is faster and cheaper with less armor; the Soviet **Rhino** is slower, tougher, and hits harder, at a higher price. Same slot, opposite profile, and that single pairing telegraphs each faction's entire personality.

### Aircraft

Aircraft are the trickiest domain because they ignore terrain, which means they ignore most of your map design. Games handle this three ways:

1. **Ammo/rearm loops** — aircraft must return to a pad to reload (C&C standard). This caps their sustained damage and gives the defender a window.
2. **Hard AA counters** — dedicated AA units delete air so efficiently that air is a punish tool, not a strategy.
3. **Restricting them to support powers** — Tempest Rising made fixed-wing aircraft *support powers only*, with helicopters and drones as the buildable air roster. Elegant solution.

| Slot | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Scout / spotter drone | Vision, cheap, detection | Spy Drone, Combat Drone, Overlord |
| Gunship | Anti-ground attack helicopter | Queller, Comanche, Apache |
| Interceptor / air superiority | Kills other aircraft | Raptor, Sea Scorpion (naval AA) |
| Bomber | Base-breaker, one big pass | Condor, Harrier, Aurora, Stealth Bomber |
| Transport | Airlifts infantry, drops | Skycrane, Chinook, Nighthawk |
| Heavy airship | Slow flying fortress | Kirov Airship |
| Air support platform | Drone carrier, jammer, detector | Drone Control Unit |

**Design note:** if a faction has *no* air units, that's a legitimate identity (GLA). But it needs enormous compensation — GLA got the best stealth, the fastest ground army, self-rebuilding structures, and the ability to scavenge weapon upgrades from kills.

### Naval

See Part 6 for whether you should include this at all. The template, if you do — this is RA2's, which is about as clean as the genre gets:

| Slot | Function | Allied | Soviet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface combatant | Ship-to-ship, shore bombardment | Destroyer | Typhoon Attack Sub |
| Naval AA | Air defense umbrella | Aegis Cruiser | Sea Scorpion |
| Naval siege | Long-range strikes on land | Aircraft Carrier | Dreadnought |
| Sub-hunter / detector | Finds and kills submerged units | Dolphin (+ Destroyer's Osprey) | — |
| Ambusher / stealth | Strikes from concealment | (submarine role) | Giant Squid |
| Transport | Amphibious landing | Hovercraft | Amphibious Transport |

Note the asymmetry: the Allies get **surface supremacy and detection**, the Soviets get **submerged ambush**. The Allied Destroyer carries its own ASW aircraft; the Soviets counter with stealth and the Giant Squid, which grapples ships. That's the same five slots producing two completely different naval feels.

## Part 4 — The counter system

### Hard vs soft counters

This is the most consequential decision you'll make, and it's a dial, not a switch.

> **Where we set the dial (see `docs/factions/counter-matrix.md`).** ADR 0005 targets ~even matchups with no capability gaps, which pushes us toward the **soft-with-cliffs** end: everything can hurt everything (no true 0% cell on a valid target), but there are pronounced bonuses and penalties so scouting and composition still matter. Hard 400%/25% swings would recreate the select-screen coin flip ADR 0005 exists to avoid.

- **Hard counters** (a rocket trooper does 400% to tanks, 25% to infantry) reward **scouting, tech choice, and composition**. Battles are decided before they start.
- **Soft counters** (everything hurts everything, some things a bit more) reward **micro, positioning, and terrain**. Battles are decided as they happen.

Most shipped games mix them. C&C leans hard; StarCraft II is softer than its reputation; Company of Heroes uses a penetration model that's soft-with-cliffs.

**Rule of thumb:** the harder your counters, the more you must give the player *information*. Hard counters plus fog of war plus no scouting tools equals a coin flip.

### Armor/damage class systems

The standard implementation is a matrix of **damage types × armor types**. Warcraft III's is the reference implementation: 7 attack types (Normal, Pierce, Siege, Magic, Chaos, Spells, Hero) against 6 armor types (Light, Medium, Heavy, Fortified, Hero, Unarmored), with each pairing having its own multiplier. Normal does 150% vs Medium; Pierce does double vs Light.

For a modern/sci-fi setting, a compact version:

**Armor classes (5):**
| Class | Applies to |
|---|---|
| Infantry | Foot soldiers |
| Light | Scouts, APCs, buggies, helicopters, light boats |
| Heavy | MBTs, superheavies, capital ships |
| Air | All aircraft (or fold into Light/Heavy) |
| Structure | Buildings and defenses |

**Damage types (6):**
| Type | Strong vs | Weak vs |
|---|---|---|
| Small arms | Infantry | Heavy, Structure |
| Autocannon | Light, Air | Heavy |
| AP / kinetic | Heavy, Light | Infantry |
| Explosive / AoE | Infantry, Structure | Air (can't target) |
| Missile | Air, Heavy | Infantry (overkill, inefficient) |
| Energy / exotic | *Faction-flavored* | — |

Five armor × six damage is 30 cells. That's enough expressiveness without being unreadable. **Whatever matrix you build, expose it in-game.** Players in games with hidden counter matrices reliably complain about exactly this.

### The two golden rules

1. **Every unit must be hard-countered by something that costs less than it does.** This is what stops any single unit from being the answer. The Overlord Tank is terrifying — and dies to massed basic rocket infantry from either opposing faction.
2. **Every unit must have a job no other unit in its own faction does better.** If two of your units overlap, one is dead weight and the other is mandatory. Cut or redesign.

## Part 5 — Making factions unique without making them overpowered

This is the actual question you're asking. There are six techniques, roughly in ascending order of how much identity they create and how much balance risk they carry.

### 1. Same slot, different stat profile (low risk, low identity)
Both factions have an MBT. One is fast/cheap/fragile, the other slow/expensive/tough. Grizzly vs Rhino. This is your baseline for ~60% of slots and it's safe because the cost curve does the balancing.

### 2. Different delivery of the same effect (low risk, good identity)
Both factions can heal infantry. One builds a Medic that follows the squad; the other builds a Propaganda Tower that passively heals in a radius (China). Same outcome, completely different play pattern — and it changes how each faction plays the map, because one is mobile and one is positional.

### 3. Role compression (medium risk, high identity)
One faction gets a unit that does two jobs. RA2's Allied **IFV** changes weapon based on which infantry rides inside — one chassis, a dozen roles. This is powerful identity but dangerous: compressed units tend to become auto-include. Price them like the two units they replace, not one.

### 4. Missing slot + compensation (high risk, highest identity)

> **We do not use this one (ADR 0005).** Removing an *answer* — the thing this technique does — is exactly the capability hole we reject: it decides a matchup at the select screen. We get the identity it promises from techniques #1–#3 and #5 instead (re-profiling the same answer, distinctive-but-deniable delivery, one owned verb). A *pure option* that answers no strategy (a ground superheavy, a specific superweapon) may still be absent on one faction — that's not a hole. Kept here as genre context.

GLA has no aircraft. Tempest Rising has no navy for anyone. Zerg has no true artillery. **This is the single strongest identity tool in the genre** and also the one most likely to go wrong. If you do it:
- The hole must be *visible* to the player from the faction description
- The compensation must be in a *different currency* (GLA got speed, stealth, economy, and structure resilience — not "better tanks")
- The hole must be exploitable by opponents in a way that's fun to defend against, not just an auto-loss on certain maps

### 5. Mechanic ownership (medium risk, very high identity)
Only one faction gets a mechanic. Soviets get mind control (Yuri). GDF get an **Intel** resource generated by combat that gates their advanced units. Veti **summon** their buildings rather than constructing them, and farm their resource sustainably so it never depletes. Dynasty are willing to sacrifice their own troops as a *mechanic*, not just flavor.

This is my recommendation for your third faction. Give it a verb no other faction has.

### 6. Economy asymmetry (high risk, high identity)
Different resource models per faction. USA gathers with fast Chinooks carrying large loads; GLA scavenges wrecks. Powerful, but it means every balance change ripples through the entire game. Do this last, if at all.

### The power budget

A unit's total strength = raw stats + utility + tempo. If you give it a unique ability, **take stats away**. The most common indie RTS failure is giving a faction's unit a cool ability *on top of* comparable stats "so it feels good," then discovering that faction wins 65% of matchups.

Concretely: if unit A and unit B are both tier-2 MBTs at the same cost and A gets a stun ability, A should have roughly 10–20% less effective HP or DPS. Write down the budget before you write the unit.

## Part 6 — Naval design

Naval is the domain where RTS rosters usually die. It dies for one reason: it becomes a **parallel game**. A second economy, fought with a second army, whose only payoff is not losing the second economy. Players correctly conclude that the optimal move is to spend as little on ships as they can survive with, and the whole domain becomes a tax.

Everything in this section exists to prevent that. The design thesis is:

> **Naval is not a second front. It is a support layer for the land game.**

If a player can point at their fleet and say *"that's why my land push worked"*, naval is earning its place. If they can only say *"that's why I didn't lose the water"*, it isn't.

Four systems do the work: **shore bombardment with remote spotting**, **carriers as mobile air infrastructure**, **offshore economy**, and **amphibious cross-pollination**. The rest is plumbing.

---

### 6.1 Shore bombardment and the spotting chain

This is the single most important naval mechanic in the design. It's what converts naval superiority into land-game pressure.

**The chain:** a friendly unit anywhere on the map has vision of a target → any bombardment ship in range can fire on it → shells arrive after a delay.

The spotter can be anything — a recon drone, an infantry scout, a stealthed operative, a hovering UAV. The ship never needs its own line of sight. This makes offshore fire a **multi-domain combo**, which is exactly the cross-pollination that makes water feel connected to the rest of the game rather than walled off from it.

#### Rules that make it work

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Spotting is automatic** | If any friendly unit has vision, the ship can fire. Never make the player manually designate a target — the skill should be keeping the spotter alive, not clicking. |
| **Shell flight time: 2–3 seconds** | Shells land where the target *was*. Devastating against static bases and sieging blobs, nearly useless against a micro'd army. This is what makes bombardment a **siege tool, not a battlefield tool**. |
| **Minimum range** | Bombardment ships cannot hit the shoreline itself. Without this, no one can build within a screen of water and half your maps become unplayable. |
| **Coastal batteries outrange ships** | The static defense that counters bombardment must have *more* range than the ship. Realistic (shore guns are stable platforms with better fire control) and it's your primary balance valve. |
| **No self-defense** | Bombardment ships should be near-helpless in a knife fight. They are artillery that floats. |

#### The range ladder

Get this ordering right and everything else falls into place. Using arbitrary units where the standard MBT has range 10:

| Range | Unit |
|---|---|
| 10 | Main battle tank |
| 14 | Land artillery |
| 16 | Standard base defense turret |
| 22 | **Bombardment ship** — outranges every base defense |
| 28 | **Coastal battery** (static, naval-only targets, expensive) |

The bombardment ship outranges all conventional defenses. The *only* static structure that answers it is the coastal battery — which is naval-only, so it's a dedicated, visible, expensive commitment the defender must choose to make. That's a real decision rather than a passive counter.

Supreme Commander proves the concept: the Cybran Salem Class Destroyer's proton cannon has range 80 against the UEF's tier-3 Ravager point defense at 70 — a tier-2 naval unit outranging a tier-3 land defense, which is precisely why it's worth bringing to an assault.

#### Counter-recon becomes a real activity

The nicest consequence of automatic spotting is that **killing the enemy's scout suddenly matters a lot**. A single cheap drone is the difference between a fleet that can hit your base and a fleet that can't. You get an entire gameplay loop — hunting spotters, escorting your own, contesting vision inland — for free, and it links the naval and land games together at the information layer.

### 6.2 Carriers as mobile air infrastructure

Most RTS carriers are siege units that happen to launch planes (RA2's Aircraft Carrier is functionally a Dreadnought with extra steps). Yours should be a **station** — it moves the launch point for your existing air force. That's a genuinely different verb and a rarer one.

| Design call | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Extend or enable? | **Extend.** Land airbases still work everywhere. | If the carrier is a prerequisite for air play on water maps, it's a mandatory build. Mandatory builds are dead decisions. |
| What does it actually give? | Rearm/repair pads + extended operational radius | Solves the C&C rearm-loop problem *geographically*. Air range becomes a map-control question. |
| Self-defense? | Near-zero. Point-defense AA only. | Forces an escort. The escort requirement is what makes carrier play interesting. |
| What happens when it sinks? | Docked aircraft die. Airborne aircraft get a **bingo-fuel timer** to reach any friendly pad. | Produces a genuinely dramatic scramble. High-value target without being an "I lose my air force" button. |
| Capacity | 4–6 aircraft docked | Enough to matter, not enough to be your whole air force. |

**Optional flourish:** let the carrier's deck be visible — show docked aircraft on it. It's a free readability win. The enemy can scout the carrier and count the planes.

### 6.3 Submarines and the detection economy

The submarine's job in this design is **cheap deterrence** — it lets a land-focused player buy insurance against a fleet without building a fleet of their own. That's a real and valuable role, and it lives or dies on how you price detection.

#### The critical rule: detection is a unit, not an upgrade

If anti-submarine detection is a researched upgrade, subs stop functioning the moment anyone clicks it and the money spent on them was wasted. If detection lives in **hulls** — a destroyer with sonar, a patrol boat with a dipping sonar, an ASW helicopter — then it costs supply, occupies a build slot, and can be killed. That's a cat-and-mouse layer instead of an on/off switch.

Precedent: Supreme Commander destroyers carry sonar, torpedoes *and* anti-torpedo defenses in one hull. RA2's Allied Destroyer carried an Osprey aircraft that engaged submerged units automatically and respawned free if shot down.

#### The other critical rule: subs surface to hit land

A submerged sub may only attack naval targets. To strike anything on land, it must surface first — which reveals it and creates the vulnerability window. Without this rule you haven't built a submarine, you've built invisible artillery.

#### Target numbers

| Matchup | Intended outcome |
|---|---|
| Sub vs unescorted surface ship | Sub wins roughly **1.5:1 by cost** |
| Sub vs dedicated ASW hull | Sub loses badly |
| Sub vs land target | Must surface; loses to any coastal defense |
| Sub vs sub | Roughly even; whoever spots first wins |

That math is what makes "buy three subs as insurance" a legitimate strategy without making it dominant.

### 6.4 Transports

The split you want is clean:

| | Air transport | Sea transport |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Capacity | 2–4 infantry, or 1 light vehicle | 8–12 units, **including superheavies** |
| Survivability | Fragile, dies to any AA | Armored, but a huge visible target |
| Counterplay | Intercept in the air | See it coming, scramble a defense |

The strategic moment you're designing for — landing a superheavy on an undefended flank — is one of the best things an RTS can produce. The risk is that the loss feels so catastrophic nobody attempts it.

**Don't fix this by making the transport tanky.** Slow, visible and vulnerable is *correct*. Fix it by making the **escort game** the fun part: the defender should see the landing coming and have to respond, and the attacker should have to protect the crossing. That's a readable, fair, dramatic interaction. What you want to avoid is a single aircraft deleting the transport with no warning — which is exactly why fleet AA (§6.6) is load-bearing.

Sea transports should also be able to unload onto any shoreline, not just at designated docks. Docked-only unloading is where transports become tedious.

---

### 6.5 Amphibious and walking ships

Cross-domain units are the strongest tool for making water feel connected rather than partitioned. Supreme Commander's Cybran **Salem Class Destroyer** deploys six spider legs and crawls ashore (in SupCom 2 it's an upgrade called LEGS — Land Emergence Galleon System). Three lessons:

**Steal the range profile.** The Salem out-ranges every point defense in the game, a full tier above its own. That's what makes crawling ashore worth doing — it isn't a land unit, it's a siege engine arriving from an unexpected direction.

**Steal the balance lever.** The Salem has the *lowest armor of the three factions' destroyers* and lower DPS than the UEF's. It pays for its amphibious trick in straight-fight performance, so it loses at sea to purpose-built warships. Write that trade down explicitly — this is the power budget from Part 5, applied.

**Avoid the pathfinding trap.** At launch the Salem was roughly 4× faster on water than on land, but pathfinding just took the shortest route, so it would haul itself over an isthmus when sailing around would have been faster. The shipped fix: **it only moves onto land if the player explicitly places a waypoint on land.** Build that rule in from day one rather than patching it later.

**Give it to exactly one faction.** If all three have walking ships, it isn't identity — it's a pathfinding bill you pay three times.

Beyond the walking ship, a general amphibious tier is worth having across all factions: hover MBTs, amphibious APCs, engineers that can cross water. Anything that traverses both domains reduces the land/water partition pain enormously.

### 6.6 The naval slot list

Two gaps are common in first drafts of a naval roster and both are fatal: **fleet anti-air** and a **cheap tier-1 hull**. Marked below.

| Tier | Slot | Job | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Corvette / patrol boat** ⚠ | Cheap screen, scout, contests early water | **Essential.** Without it, entry cost to the naval game is a destroyer, and "defend without over-investing" fails at the first hurdle |
| T1 | Light transport | Early amphibious raids | 4 infantry |
| T2 | **Destroyer** | Surface combat + ASW + light shore bombardment | The naval MBT. Everything is priced against it |
| T2 | **Submarine** | Anti-ship ambush, cheap deterrence | Must surface to hit land |
| T2 | **AA cruiser** ⚠ | Fleet air defense umbrella | **Essential.** Carriers, bombardment ships and transports are all soft high-value targets. Without this slot, fleets evaporate to air and the domain collapses |
| T2 | Heavy transport | Bulk landings, carries superheavies | 8–12 capacity |
| T3 | **Bombardment ship** | Long-range shore strikes via spotters | The payoff unit. Helpless up close |
| T3 | **Carrier** | Mobile air station | Near-zero self-defense |
| T3 | Missile submarine | Surfaces to strike land, submerges to escape | High-risk, high-reward |
| — | **Coastal battery** (structure) | Static, outranges bombardment ships | Naval targets only. Expensive |
| — | **Shipyard** (structure) | Produces naval units | Placed on shoreline |
| — | **Sea platform** (structure) | Power, defenses, expansion built on water | Makes water feel like territory |

That's **9 hulls per faction**, which is a real cost. See §6.9 on scope.

#### Naval armor and damage classes

Extend the Part 4 matrix rather than building a separate one:

| Armor class | Applies to |
|---|---|
| Light naval | Corvettes, transports, submarines (surfaced) |
| Heavy naval | Destroyers, cruisers, carriers, bombardment ships |
| Submerged | Any sub while submerged |

| Damage type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Torpedo** | Only type that can hit `Submerged`. Strong vs Heavy naval, weak vs Light naval (they're fast and shallow-draft) |
| Naval gun | Strong vs Heavy naval and Structure, poor vs Light naval and aircraft |
| Autocannon / CIWS | Strong vs Light naval and Air, useless vs Heavy naval |
| Anti-ship missile | Strong vs Heavy naval, cannot target Submerged |

The load-bearing rule: **`Submerged` is only targetable by torpedoes and depth charges, and only by units with detection.** That's the entire submarine game in one line.

---

### 6.7 Offshore economy — oil sites and rigs

Without this, naval superiority buys you nothing but not-losing-the-water, and the optimal strategy becomes minimal naval spend. Offshore income is what makes the sea worth fighting over.

For a semi-realistic setting, oil is the right fit. There are two models, and they behave very differently.

#### Model A — buildable rigs on neutral oil sites

The map contains **oil sites** — permanent, neutral, marked terrain features in the water. A site produces nothing on its own. A player sends a construction unit and builds an **oil rig** on it, which then generates passive income.

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Site | Permanent map furniture. **Indestructible.** Visible to all players from match start |
| Rig | Buildable structure. **Destructible.** Roughly 60–90 seconds to construct |
| Income | Passive drip, no harvester round-trip required |
| Payback period | **4–6 minutes** (see below) |
| On destruction | Rig dies, site remains. Anyone can rebuild |

**The key design property is that the site is indestructible and the rig is not.** Destroying the rig is a setback, not a permanent kill. The contest is *renewable* — the same fight happens again in three minutes. This is what stops offshore economy from being a single decisive moment that ends the game.

**Payback period is your main dial.** If a rig pays for itself in 2 minutes, building one is a no-brainer and there's no decision. If it takes 8, nobody bothers. Target **4–6 minutes**: long enough that committing is a real risk, short enough that it's worth defending.

#### Model B — capturable pre-built rigs

The rig already exists on the map, neutral, and an engineer captures it. This is C&C Generals' Oil Derrick model. Faster to contest, less investment, flips back and forth more.

**Model A is the better fit for your design**, because the build step is a *commitment* the opponent can scout and punish, and because it gives your engineer/construction unit a naval job. Model B is better for high-tempo games where you want objectives flipping every 90 seconds.

You can also mix them: buildable sites in the corners, one capturable pre-built rig in the centre.

#### The single central rig

Your instinct here is right, and it produces one of the best map archetypes in the genre — a king-of-the-hill objective that structures the whole match. But it carries a specific danger.

**The snowball problem:** whoever takes the central rig gets more income → builds more navy → holds the rig harder → gets more income. That's an uncorrected positive feedback loop and it can decide games at the 6-minute mark.

Four fixes, use at least two:

1. **Holding costs something.** The rig needs escort or defenses to survive. It generates income but consumes attention and units. A rig you're not defending is a rig you're about to lose.
2. **The rig is destructible, the site is not.** The loser can always deny rather than being locked out. Denial is cheaper than capture, which is a healthy asymmetry — the player who's behind has a cheap play available.
3. **Diminishing returns on multiple rigs.** Second rig gives 100%, third gives 70%, fourth gives 50%. Caps the runaway without hard-limiting anything.
4. **Central rig income is high but the site is exposed.** Place it in open water with no cover, reachable by both players' air and navy. Big prize, indefensible position. It should be contested constantly, never *held*.

**Always show offshore sites on the minimap to everyone from match start.** The central rig should produce a *scheduled fight*, not an ambush. Both players know it's there, both know it's coming, and the interesting decision is whether to contest it now or build up first.

#### Should oil be a second currency?

**No.** Keep it as the same credit currency, but with a different *acquisition model* — passive drip versus active harvester round-trip. That gives you all the strategic flavour (offshore income is safe from raiding but exposed to naval attack; harvester income is the reverse) with none of the balance complexity of a dual-resource economy. This is the C&C Generals oil derrick model and it works.

### 6.8 Map design rules

Naval only works if the maps cooperate. These are non-negotiable:

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Water share: 25–40%** | Below 25% and ships are irrelevant sunk cost. Above 40% and ground assaults — which usually close out games — become miserable |
| **Shallow water** | Hybrid terrain traversable by light hulls *and* ground units. Big ships restricted to deep water. Wargame: Red Dragon does this well |
| **Every base touches water** | Or naval is a per-map coin flip. If you want land-only maps, they should be an explicit, labelled subset |
| **No water-locked victory** | It must always be possible to win via land. Water is leverage, not a win condition |
| **Offshore sites near contested space** | Not tucked safely behind either player's spawn |
| **Bigger maps** | Naval works far better at Supreme Commander scale than StarCraft scale. Longer traversal times mean overcommitting to one domain is less instantly fatal |

---

### 6.9 Scope consequences

Committing to naval properly is not a bolt-on. Be honest about what it costs:

- **+9 hulls per faction** — 27 more units to model, animate, balance, and voice
- **+3 naval structures** (shipyard, coastal battery, sea platform)
- **Naval pathfinding** — a genuinely hard problem, especially with amphibious units
- **A second AI layer** — the AI must contest water, or it's a free win on every naval map
- **Map pipeline complexity** — shallow/deep water, shoreline handling, offshore site placement

Roughly a **25–30% increase in content and balance scope** over a three-domain game.

If you need to cut, cut in this order:

1. **Missile submarine** (T3) — the plain submarine already covers deterrence
2. **Light transport** (T1) — the heavy transport can cover both if you soften the tier gate
3. **Sea platforms** — nice for feel, not load-bearing
4. **Carrier** — painful, but the bombardment ship is the mechanic that actually justifies naval

**Do not cut, under any circumstances:** the corvette, the AA cruiser, the coastal battery, or offshore income. Each of those is the fix for a specific failure mode described above, and removing any one of them reintroduces the problem it was solving.

## Part 8 — Practical rules of thumb

**On roster size**
- 20–26 buildable units per faction is the sweet spot
- 3 tech tiers is standard; 4 makes games too long
- ~60–70% of slots should be mirrored across factions with different profiles; ~30–40% genuinely unique
- Players will reliably use 6–8 unit types in any given engagement regardless of how many you give them

**On unit design**
- Write the unit's *job* in one sentence before you write a single stat. If you can't, cut it.
- Every unit needs a stated hard counter that costs less than it does
- No two units in the same faction should have the same job
- Cool ability = reduced stats. Always. Write the budget down.

**On testing**
- The MBT mirror is your baseline — tune that first, price everything against it
- Build the counter matrix as a spreadsheet before you build the units
- If a unit is never built after 20 hours of playtesting, it isn't underpowered, it's redundant

**On scope**
- Four domains is a ~25–30% scope increase over three. Commit fully or not at all — half-realised naval is worse than none
- If naval is in, the corvette, AA cruiser, coastal battery and offshore income are non-negotiable (see 6.9)
- Ship the third faction after launch if you need to (Tempest Rising did exactly this with the Veti)
- Superweapons are content, not balance — design the game to work without them
